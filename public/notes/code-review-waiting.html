The real cost of code review was the waiting

November 2026, 3 min read

When I stepped into a tech lead role, the first thing I wanted to understand was where our delivery time actually went. So I looked at the data.

I went through our team's history: hundreds of pull requests and more than a thousand review comments. The finding surprised me.

Reviewing wasn't the problem

Very few pull requests ever had changes requested. The time went into the queue: almost every pull request sat waiting for someone to pick it up. We were paying the full cost of a careful review process on changes that rarely needed one.

Match review effort to risk

That led to two proposals, which the team is still working through:

  • Risk-based review levels. A throwaway spike doesn't need the same review as a first production launch. Define a few levels, from no review for experiments up to a full review before anything ships to production, and let the level decide the process.
  • Standards short enough to follow. Our coding standards had grown into a long set of documents that neither people nor coding agents could realistically hold in their heads. The proposal condenses them into a short always-on core, rules scoped to the parts of the codebase they apply to, and checks in CI, so the standards are enforced by tooling instead of in review comments.

The general lesson

Speed and quality are usually framed as a trade-off. Most of the time, the real cost is a process that applies the same weight to every change. Invest in architecture and guardrails up front, then let people move fast inside them.
